  Let us make this more explicit. For the trilinear vertex, put the points on
the sphere at the standard 0, 1, ∞ and choose coordinate frames z (0) , z (1) ,
and z (∞) at each, so the three sewing circles are |z (0,1,∞) | = 1. The choice
should be invariant (up to phase) under the six Möbius transformations
that permute the vertex operators, so that the vertex is symmetric. There
are many possible choices, for example

As q runs over the unit disk, z4 covers the outer shaded region of fig-
ure 9.6(b). Permuting the external vertex operators gives the other two
regions. We could try to take a smaller, so the shaded regions grow, but
they will eventually overlap and double-count some surfaces while other
surfaces will still not be produced. They will never precisely fill the plane,
so we need to add in an explicit four-point vertex V0,4 which is given
by the sphere with four marked points, integrated over the unshaded part
of the z-plane. This does not define V0,4 completely because there is
also a choice of coordinate at each puncture for each value of z4 . It is
always possible to make this choice in such a way as to connect smoothly
at the boundary between the shaded and unshaded regions. Note that
the Feynman graph of figure 9.6(a) includes all the asymptotic regions
z4 → 0, 1, ∞, while the four-point vertex includes only nondegenerate sur-
faces. The behavior in the limits is then given explicitly by the q → 0 limit
of the plumbing fixture: this is why this construction is useful.
   As another example, sewing two of the circles of a single vertex to the
ends of a cylinder produces a torus with one vertex operator. This covers
the limit Im τ → ∞ of the moduli space of the torus, but an interior region
is missed and must be added by hand as a vertex region V1,1 .
   This construction can be extended inductively. The sewing operation
always increases the parameter
