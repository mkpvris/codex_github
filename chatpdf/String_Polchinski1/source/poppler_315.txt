   Although the Schottky and Fuchsian parameterizations are similar,
they have rather different properties. In particular, the complex structure

                               The period matrix

The modulus τ of the torus has a natural g × g matrix generalization at
genus g. To see this, we need first the Abelian differentials or holomorphic
one-forms. These are world-sheet fields /a with the property that in
complex coordinates
                             ∂z̄ /z = 0 ,       /z̄ = 0 .                   (9.2.5)
There are precisely g such fields, and g more satisfying the conjugate
relation. This follows from the Riemann–Roch theorem (5.3.22). The
Abelian differentials (9.2.5) and their conjugates are just the kernel of
the operator P0T , for which the Riemann–Roch theorem is
                  dim ker P0T − dim ker P0 = −χ = 2g − 2 .                  (9.2.6)
The operator P0 acts on scalar fields c and c̃ with h = 0, and so reduces
to the ordinary gradient. This always has two zero modes (c = constant
or c̃ = constant), and so dim ker P0T = 2g.
  The line integral of a one-form is a coordinate-invariant quantity. A
natural basis for the Abelian differentials is then /zi , i = 1, . . . , g, defined
by

                                      dz /zj = δij .                        (9.2.7)

Then the period matrix

characterizes the complex structure of the surface. At genus one, where
/z is constant, this is the usual τ.
   It can be shown that τij is symmetric, so it has 12 g(g + 1) complex
parameters. For g = 1, 2, 3 this is equal to the number of complex mod-
uli, but for g ≥ 4 it is greater, so not all period matrices correspond
to Riemann surfaces. The theta function (7.2.36) has a natural genus-g
generalization, where τ becomes the period matrix and ν, n, a, b become
g-component vectors.
